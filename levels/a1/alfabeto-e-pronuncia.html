L'Alfabeto e la Pronuncia

Italian spelling is famously regular — learn the handful of rules that make it so.

ObiettiviSpiegazioneRegoleEsempiErrori ComuniPraticaRiepilogoContinua

Introduzione

Italian is written almost exactly as it sounds. Once you know a small set of spelling rules, you can read any Italian word aloud correctly — even one you've never seen.

Al termine di questa lezione saprai…

  • Say the 21 letters of the Italian alphabet
  • Pronounce c/g before different vowels correctly
  • Recognize gn, gl and sc as single sounds

Spiegazione

Spiegazione

The Italian alphabet has 21 native letters (no j, k, w, x, y — these appear only in loanwords like jeans or whisky). Every vowel (a, e, i, o, u) is always pronounced clearly and the same way — Italian has no silent vowels and no vowel reduction like English “the” becoming “thuh”.

The trickiest part for English speakers is that c and g change sound depending on the letter that follows them, and a few two/three-letter combinations represent a single sound.

Regole Grammaticali

Le Regole

a) C and G before vowels

  • ca, co, cu = hard "k" sound — casa, cosa, cubo
  • ce, ci = soft "ch" sound (like English "cheese") — cena, cinema
  • ga, go, gu = hard "g" sound — gatto, gonna, guanto
  • ge, gi = soft "j" sound (like English "jet") — gelato, giorno

b) Keeping the hard/soft sound before e or i

  • Add an h to keep the hard sound before e/i: che, chi = "k" (chi, "who"); ghe, ghi = hard "g" (spaghetti)
  • Add an i to keep the soft sound before a/o/u: cia, cio, ciu = "ch" (ciao); gia, gio, giu = "j" (giorno) — the i is silent here, just a spelling signal

c) Special combinations

  • gn = "ny" as in canyon — gnocchi, bagno
  • gli = "ly" as in million — famiglia, figlio
  • sc before e/i = "sh" — pesce, sciare; before a/o/u = "sk" — scuola, pesca
  • Double consonants are held longer than single ones — sono (I am) vs. sonno (sleep) are genuinely different words

Esempi

Vediamola in Pratica

  • Ciao, come ti chiami?
    Hi, what's your name? — ciao starts with the soft "ch" sound
  • Ho fame, voglio la pasta.
    I'm hungry, I want pasta — voglio has a soft "ly"-like gli is not here, but voglio's gli combo shows the pattern in famiglia
  • Il gatto dorme sul letto.
    The cat sleeps on the bed — gatto has the hard "g" sound
  • Mia figlia studia all'università.
    My daughter studies at university — figlia uses the gli "ly" sound
  • Gli spaghetti sono buonissimi.
    The spaghetti is delicious — spaghetti keeps the hard "g" thanks to the h

Usa invece"ciao" with a soft "ch" sound (chow)

ci before a vowel is always the soft "ch" sound in Italian, unlike the hard c English speakers expect.

EvitaTreating "sono" and "sonno" as the same word

Usa invecesono (I am / they are) vs. sonno (sleep, drowsiness) — genuinely different

Double consonants change the meaning of the word, not just the spelling — they must be held longer in speech.

Ripasso
